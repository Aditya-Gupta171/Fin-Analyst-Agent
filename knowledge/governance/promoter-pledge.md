# Promoter pledges and encumbrances

## What it measures
Promoters sometimes pledge their shares as collateral for loans taken by themselves or by group companies. The
pledged share of promoter holding shows how much of the controlling stake is exposed to lender action. SEBI's
takeover regulations treat pledges, liens, non-disposal undertakings and similar arrangements as encumbrances that
promoters must disclose.

## How to read it
- If the share price falls, lenders can ask for more collateral or sell pledged shares, which pushes the price
  down further and can trigger a change in control.
- High pledging indicates financial need at the promoter level, sometimes to fund other group businesses.
- The trend matters: rising pledges while the company reports strong results is a mismatch worth investigating.
- Non-disposal undertakings can hide pledge-like arrangements; read the encumbrance disclosure, not only "pledge".

## Red flags
- More than about a quarter of promoter holding encumbered; more than half is severe.
- Pledges increasing over consecutive quarters.
- Invocation of pledges by lenders disclosed to exchanges.
- Promoter group entities borrowing from the listed company while promoters pledge shares.

## Benign explanations
- Pledges created to secure borrowings of the listed company itself, disclosed as such, and declining with
  repayment.

## Where to find it in Indian filings
- Quarterly shareholding pattern filed with stock exchanges, which shows shares pledged or otherwise encumbered.
- Encumbrance disclosures by promoters under the SEBI Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers Regulations.
- Offer documents: "Capital Structure" discloses pledged or encumbered promoter shares; minimum promoter
  contribution must be locked in and cannot be pledged except in limited circumstances.

## Questions for management
- Why are promoter shares encumbered, and for whose borrowings?
- What is the plan to reduce the encumbrance?
