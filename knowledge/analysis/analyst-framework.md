# How a financial analyst reviews a filing

## What it measures
This is the working method behind every analysis: what to look at first, how to decide what matters, and how to
write a finding that another analyst would accept. It applies to annual reports, quarterly results and offer
documents alike.

## How to read it
1. **Context first.** Identify the business model, sector, size, basis (standalone or consolidated), reporting
   period and document type. The same number means different things for a bank, an EPC contractor and a consumer
   brand.
2. **Can the numbers be trusted?** Check that statements reconcile, the auditor's opinion is unmodified, and there is
   no going-concern uncertainty. Hold back conclusions that depend on numbers that fail these checks.
3. **Is profit real?** Compare profit with operating cash flow over several periods; separate operating profit from
   other income and exceptional items; check the tax rate.
4. **What does the balance sheet carry?** Working capital intensity and trend, leverage and debt service, capital
   work-in-progress, contingent liabilities.
5. **Who benefits?** Related party transactions, promoter pledges, auditor changes, customer concentration.
6. **For offer documents, is the deal fair?** Who receives the money, how it will be used, the valuation against
   peers and past transactions, and litigation.
7. **Synthesise.** Rank findings by materiality and by how strongly the evidence supports them, not by how many
   rules fired. Several weak signals pointing the same way (receivables up, cash conversion down, credit loss
   allowance flat) outweigh one strong signal with a plausible explanation.

Standards for a finding:
- Every figure is cited to a reported fact or a computed metric.
- The finding states why it matters, in business terms, not just which threshold was crossed.
- Benign explanations available in the filing have been considered and either ruled out or acknowledged.
- It ends with a specific question management could answer.
- Materiality is explicit: a governance issue can matter even when small in rupees; a large variance can be
  immaterial if it is a known timing effect.

## Red flags
- Patterns across areas matter more than isolated ratios: aggressive growth plus weak cash conversion plus related
  party revenue plus an auditor change is a far stronger picture than any one of them.
- Disclosures that become less detailed over time, or explanations that change from year to year.
- Management commentary that emphasises adjusted metrics while statutory numbers deteriorate.

## Benign explanations
- Every signal has innocent causes; the analyst's job is to test which explanation the evidence supports, not to
  assume the worst. Seasonality, growth phases, acquisitions and accounting changes are the most common.

## Where to find it in Indian filings
- Financial statements and notes, the auditor's report, MD&A, the directors' report and corporate governance
  report; for offer documents, risk factors, restated financial information, objects of the offer, basis for offer
  price, capital structure and litigation.

## Questions for management
- Good questions are specific, answerable from the company's own records, and tied to a cited observation, for
  example: "How much of the year-end receivables had been collected by the end of the first quarter?"
