# Free cash flow — what is left for shareholders and lenders

## What it measures
Free cash flow (FCF) is operating cash flow less capital expenditure. It is the cash available to repay debt, pay
dividends, buy back shares or fund acquisitions without raising new capital.

## How to read it
- Negative FCF is normal during an investment phase; the question is whether the investment earns a return and how
  the gap is funded.
- Persistent negative FCF with no visible expansion means the business consumes cash just to operate.
- Under Ind AS 116, lease payments are financing cash flows, so operating cash flow and FCF look better than before
  adoption. For lease-heavy businesses, subtract lease principal and interest payments for a like-for-like measure.
- Compare cumulative FCF over several years with cumulative dividends and changes in net debt to see how
  shareholder payouts are really funded.

## Red flags
- Negative FCF in every year of an offer document's track record while the offer is largely an offer for sale.
- Dividends paid while FCF is negative and borrowings rise.
- FCF improvement driven by capex cuts in a business that needs reinvestment.

## Benign explanations
- A disclosed, time-bound expansion programme funded by equity or long-term debt.
- Early-stage growth companies where operating cash flow is expected to turn positive, supported by improving unit
  economics.

## Where to find it in Indian filings
- Cash flow statement: net cash from operating activities, and purchase of property, plant and equipment and
  intangibles in investing activities.
- Lease liabilities note for lease payments.
- In offer documents: MD&A cash flow discussion and the risk factor on negative cash flows where applicable.

## Questions for management
- How will the gap between operating cash flow and capex be funded over the next two years?
- What level of capex is needed simply to maintain the existing asset base?
