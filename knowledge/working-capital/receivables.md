# Trade receivables and collection risk

## What it measures
Trade receivables are amounts customers owe for goods delivered or services rendered. Days sales outstanding (DSO)
— average receivables divided by revenue, times the days in the period — expresses them as the number of days of
sales not yet collected. Comparing receivables growth with revenue growth shows whether the company is collecting
at the same pace as it is selling.

## How to read it
- Judge DSO against the sector and the company's own history, not a universal number. Consumer staples collect in
  days, IT services in two to three months, EPC and defence contractors dealing with government customers often
  take far longer.
- Receivables growing materially faster than revenue is the core warning pattern. It means either more generous
  credit terms, slower collections, or revenue being recognised earlier than cash is due.
- Year-end timing matters: a company that ships heavily in the last month of the year shows high year-end
  receivables even with normal credit terms. Averages over opening and closing balances reduce, but do not remove,
  that effect.
- Read receivables together with cash conversion; the combination of rising DSO and falling CFO / PAT is much
  stronger evidence than either alone.
- Check the expected credit loss allowance under Ind AS 109: a rising gross receivable with a flat or falling
  allowance means the company is taking on credit risk without providing for it.

## Red flags
- Receivables growth more than about 20 percentage points above revenue growth, especially in consecutive years.
- A rising share of receivables outstanding for more than six months in the Schedule III ageing schedule.
- Large receivables from related parties or from a few customers.
- Revenue spikes in the final quarter of the year followed by higher receivables and weak first-quarter sales —
  a pattern consistent with channel stuffing.
- Expected credit loss allowance falling as a percentage of overdue receivables.
- Receivables sold or discounted near year end to make the balance look lower, with recourse retained.

## Benign explanations
- A deliberate, disclosed shift to larger institutional or government customers with longer but reliable payment
  cycles.
- Entry into export markets where credit terms are longer.
- A large contract billed near year end and collected shortly after — verify with post-balance-sheet collections.
- Acquisition of a business with a different customer profile during the year.

## Where to find it in Indian filings
- Trade receivables note, including the ageing schedule Schedule III requires (undisputed and disputed, considered
  good, significant increase in credit risk, credit impaired).
- Expected credit loss policy and allowance movement in the financial instruments note.
- Related party transactions note for balances due from related parties.
- In offer documents: MD&A discussion of working capital, and risk factors about customer concentration or delayed
  payments, which are common for government-facing businesses.

## Questions for management
- What are the standard credit terms, and did they change for any major customer this year?
- How much of the year-end receivables has been collected since the balance-sheet date?
- What share of receivables is overdue beyond 180 days, and how is it provided for?
- Are any receivables due from related parties, and on what terms?
