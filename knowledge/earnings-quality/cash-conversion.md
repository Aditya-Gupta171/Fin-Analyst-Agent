# Cash conversion — do profits turn into operating cash?

## What it measures
Cash conversion compares net cash from operating activities (CFO) with profit after tax (PAT) or EBITDA. Profit is
built from accounting judgements — when revenue is recognised, how much of a receivable is doubtful, which costs
are capitalised. Operating cash flow is much harder to shape. Over time the two should track each other; when
they persistently diverge, reported profit is running ahead of economic reality.

Common measures are CFO / PAT for a single year, cumulative CFO / cumulative PAT over three to five years, and
CFO / EBITDA, which also captures taxes and working-capital absorption.

## How to read it
- A CFO / PAT ratio around or above 1x is normal for a mature, profitable company because depreciation is a
  non-cash charge that sits inside PAT.
- One weak year is rarely conclusive: a large order delivered near year end, a deliberate inventory build ahead
  of a product launch, or rapid growth can all depress a single year.
- Multi-year cumulative conversion is the stronger signal. Below about 0.6x over three years means a large share
  of reported profits is sitting in receivables, inventory, unbilled revenue, loans or capitalised costs.
- Always decompose the gap using the working-capital lines of the cash flow statement: which item absorbed cash?
- Compare direction as well as level. Profit rising while CFO falls is a sharper signal than a stable low ratio.

## Red flags
- CFO persistently below half of PAT, or negative, while the company reports profit growth.
- The gap is explained mainly by rising trade receivables or unbilled revenue rather than inventory for a
  visible growth programme.
- Large "other current assets", "loans and advances" or "other financial assets" growing alongside the gap —
  cash may be leaving through advances to related parties or vendors.
- Operating cash flow flattered by factoring or bill discounting without disclosure, or by stretching payables.
- In an offer document, negative operating cash flow in the restated years combined with an offer that is largely
  for sale by existing shareholders.

## Benign explanations
- Genuine rapid growth where working capital must be funded ahead of collections, supported by an order book and
  improving collections after year end.
- Seasonal businesses whose year end falls at a working-capital peak (for example, a sugar or agri-input company
  in March).
- A one-off tax payment or settlement that depresses CFO for a single year.
- Project businesses (EPC, defence) where milestone billing lags execution; check the unbilled revenue ageing.
- Changes in classification: under Ind AS 7 non-financial companies may present interest paid in operating or
  financing activities, which moves CFO without changing economics.

## Where to find it in Indian filings
- Statement of cash flows in the annual report or, for listed companies, the half-yearly cash flow statement that
  accompanies the September quarter results under Regulation 33 of the LODR Regulations.
- In a DRHP / RHP: the restated consolidated statement of cash flows in the Restated Financial Information, and
  the "Management's Discussion and Analysis" section which must discuss cash flows. Negative operating cash flow in
  any restated year is typically also disclosed as a risk factor.
- Schedule III ratio disclosures and working-capital notes (receivable ageing, inventory composition).

## Questions for management
- Which working-capital items absorbed operating cash this year, and when do you expect them to reverse?
- How much of the year-end receivables has been collected since the balance-sheet date?
- Has the company used bill discounting, factoring or supply-chain finance, and is it shown as borrowing?
- Have credit terms or revenue recognition policies changed during the period?
