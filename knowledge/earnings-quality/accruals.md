# Accruals and the persistence of earnings

## What it measures
Accruals are the part of profit that has not yet been received or paid in cash: revenue booked but not collected,
expenses incurred but not paid, provisions, capitalised costs. A simple balance-sheet-free measure is
(PAT − CFO) scaled by average total assets, often called the Sloan accruals ratio after research showing that
companies with high accruals tend to report weaker future earnings and returns.

## How to read it
- The ratio is most informative relative to peers in the same sector and against the company's own history.
  Capital-intensive and project businesses naturally carry higher accruals than consumer staples.
- A positive and rising ratio means an increasing share of profit is not backed by cash in the period.
- Look for what created the accrual: a receivable that will be collected next quarter is different from a provision
  reversal or a change in an estimate.
- Changes in estimates (useful lives, expected credit loss assumptions, warranty provisions) are a quiet source of
  accruals because they raise profit without any change in operations.

## Red flags
- Accruals ratio in the top decile of the sector cohort, especially when combined with weak cash conversion.
- Provision write-backs or lower expected credit loss rates contributing materially to profit growth.
- An increase in useful lives of assets or a change in depreciation method that lifts profit.
- Capitalisation of expenses (development costs, borrowing costs, "pre-operative expenses") growing faster than the
  underlying investment.

## Benign explanations
- Strong growth in a business with a long cash cycle, where accruals rise in proportion to revenue.
- Acquisition-related balance sheet changes that distort averages in the year of acquisition.
- A deliberate, disclosed change in estimate supported by technical evaluation (for example, a revised useful life
  based on an independent assessment).

## Where to find it in Indian filings
- Significant accounting policies and "use of estimates and judgements" notes in the financial statements.
- Notes on provisions (Ind AS 37), expected credit loss on trade receivables (Ind AS 109), and property, plant and
  equipment (useful lives).
- Auditor's key audit matters (SA 701), which frequently cover revenue recognition, impairment and provisions.

## Questions for management
- What drove the difference between profit and operating cash flow this year?
- Were any estimates (useful lives, credit loss rates, warranty or other provisions) revised, and what was the
  impact on profit?
- What costs were capitalised during the year and on what basis?
